Openings, materials and parts

walnut opens things the way furniture does: four openings taken from mid-century pieces, and materials that give them weight. Then seven parts that close the gaps a walnut site used to fill with a script or a second library. Every demo runs on the built walnut.css and the browser's own elements.

How walnut opens things, 1 of 4

Plan chest

Each row is a drawer front. Pull one and the drawer slides out of the slot beneath it while the rows below make room. One drawer opens at a time, like the wide flat drawers architects keep their drawings in.

Why you would use it
A page inside your page that keeps its place in the flow: a project list, case studies, pricing, an FAQ. Nothing covers the page, so the reader never loses where they were.
On your site
<div class="wal-chest">
  <details name="work">
    <summary>Shelf Life</summary>
    <div>…the case study…</div>
  </details>
</div>
Status
Ships, pure CSS. A native <details>, so it opens with no script, find-in-page opens it, and the shared name keeps it to one drawer at a time. Under Materials it is walnut. A browser without animated details opens it at once.

Each drawer wears its own colourway. Open one, then another, then turn on Materials at the top and do it again.

How walnut opens things, 2 of 4

Sliding door

The panel runs in from the edge on a track, like the doors of an Eames storage unit. It starts with a little resistance, glides, and comes to rest on a soft close. Its leading edge carries the pull, and its shadow falls across the page.

Why you would use it
The drawer every site needs, for a menu, a basket or a case study. walnut's drawer today asks you to script focus, Escape and inert. This one is a <dialog>, so the browser does all three.
On your site
<button commandfor="menu" command="show-modal">Menu</button>

<dialog id="menu" class="wal-door" closedby="any">
  <button class="wal-door-pull" commandfor="menu"
          command="close" aria-label="Close"></button>
  <div class="wal-door-body">…</div>
</dialog>
Status
Ships, pure CSS, no script at all. commandfor opens it and closedby lets a click outside close it, so it retires .wal-drawer's three scripting chores. Under Materials it runs in heavy and knocks against its stop. On a phone it rises from the bottom.

Close it with the pull on its edge, Escape, or a click on the page.

How walnut opens things, 3 of 4

Gatefold

The card is a record sleeve. It travels to the left of the window as the cover, and the notes unfold from behind it on the spine, the way a gatefold LP opens to its inner spread.

Why you would use it
A detail page with a natural shape: the picture on one side and the words on the other. It suits case studies, products and colourways. On a phone the spread stacks and the notes fold down.
On your site
<button commandfor="forest" command="show-modal">…</button>

<dialog id="forest" class="wal-window" data-wal-open="gatefold">
  <div class="wal-gatefold-spread">
    <figure class="wal-gatefold-cover">…</figure>
    <div class="wal-gatefold-notes">…</div>
  </div>
</dialog>
<script src="walnut-motion.js" defer></script>
Status
Ships, with walnut-motion.js. Without the script it opens as a plain spread. The cover never stretches: shaped otherwise than the card's picture, it leaves cropped to the picture and opens out as it lands. Under Materials the notes come down like a heavy board and knock.

How walnut opens things, 4 of 4

Step inside

Open a colourway and you walk into it. Its colours open out of the card in the soft circle you picked for recolouring, and the page that arrives is the colourway's own, in its own palette. Back walks you out the same way.

Why you would use it
For a detail page that should feel like another room: a colourway, a collection, a project with its own brand colours. A site's palette changes and its openings then share one gesture.
On your site
<main id="page" data-wal-recolour="swatch">…</main>

walnut.recolour(page, () => showDetail("forest"), { from: card });
Status
Ships today. walnut-recolour.js already takes a function and makes any change inside the circle, and swapping the page is a change.

How walnut moves

Materials

Turn on materials and every opening moves like the thing it is made of. A walnut drawer is heavy: it runs out, strikes its stop, knocks back and settles. Brass is quick and rings as it lands. Stone is slow to start and slower to stop, and never bounces.

Why you would use it
Weight is most of what makes a piece of furniture feel made, and a screen cannot show it, only how a thing comes to rest. Motion that says what a part is made of tells the reader what to expect of it, the way the palette says what it is for.
On your site
<html data-motion="materials">

<!-- or one part, and everything inside it -->
<div class="wal-chest" data-wal-material="stone">…</div>
Status
Ships, pure CSS. On <html> it makes drawers, doors and windows walnut, and menus, pickers and charts brass. A material is four tokens, so a part of your own takes one by moving on them. Under reduced motion every opening is instant, whatever it is made of.

Each drawer holds the curve it runs on; the dashed line is its stop. Pull all three at once and watch them land, then open the second drawer of one and watch the first shut.

How walnut opens things

One way to open anything

All four open the browser's own elements, so a site chooses a movement with an attribute and keeps the same markup.

  • Native first. <details> and <dialog> bring focus, Escape, inert and find-in-page. commandfor and closedby open and close them with no script.
  • One attribute. data-wal-open picks the movement for a window, and data-motion picks how everything moves.
  • walnut-motion.js, optional. It measures the control you pressed and plays the movement. Without it every window still opens, natively, and so it does under reduced motion.
  • Your own movement. walnut.open.styles.mine = { open(dialog, from) {…}, close(dialog, from) {…} }, the same shape as your own recolour style.
  • Four tokens. Every movement runs on --wal-open-duration, --wal-open-ease and their two closing twins: glide and settle by default, a material's curves under Materials. Your CSS can run on them too.
  • For your portfolio. Sliding door makes the case-study drawer native. Gatefold gives each case study its reel as the cover and the write-up as the notes.
Glide
A drawer on ball-bearing runners: some resistance, a long run, no bump at the end.
Settle
A soft-close damper: quick travel, then the damper takes the last part.

Part two

Seven new parts

walnut themed and moved well, but a walnut site still reached for a script or a second library for a carousel, a select, a chart, a label in a colour someone picked, or a page that prints. Each of these closes one of those gaps with a class or a token.

New parts, 1 of 7

Superellipse corners

Every corner in the kit becomes a true superellipse, the curve Piet Hein drew for Stockholm's Sergels torg in 1959 and Bruno Mathsson built into his tables. One token reshapes every button, card, chip and field.

Why you would use it
A rounded rectangle is a straight edge joined to a quarter circle, and the join shows. A superellipse has no join, so the shape looks made rather than drawn. The same token gives Catalogue a bevelled corner, like a clipped index card.
On your site
:root { --wal-corner-shape: squircle; }

<section style="--wal-corner-shape: bevel">…</section>
Status
Ships. Every walnut rule that sets a radius also reads --wal-corner-shape, and the Catalogue finish bevels its corners. Where corner-shape is not supported, corners stay round.

New parts, 2 of 7

Any colour, legible

Hand walnut any colour, a brand colour or one your users picked, and it returns the four forms every cue has: the fill, the ink, the wash and the text that sits on the fill. Each passes contrast in light and in dark.

Why you would use it
Labels in a tracker, categories in a calendar, a client's colour on a case study. A yellow tag someone picks is unreadable on most sites. With .wal-tint it stays yellow and its text stays readable.
On your site
<span class="wal-chip wal-tint" style="--wal-tint: #f5d90a">Design</span>
<span class="wal-fill-tint wal-tint" style="--wal-tint: #f5d90a">Design</span>

.price { color: --wal-ink(#0a7f6f); }   /* as a CSS function */
Status
Ships. The class works wherever walnut's computed colours do. The functions use CSS @function, which Chrome and Edge have today.

    New parts, 3 of 7

    No script left

    walnut asks your script to set four state classes. The browser can now do all four, so a walnut site needs no script at all to behave.

    Why you would use it
    Fewer moving parts and nothing to keep in step. Every state is right before any script has loaded, and none of it can break when one does not.
    On your site
    <!-- the same nav as today, minus the script -->
<nav class="wal-nav">…<div class="wal-nav-links">…</div></nav>

<button interestfor="save-tip">Save</button>
<span id="save-tip" popover="hint" class="wal-tip">Saves a draft</span>
    Status
    Ships, on the existing classes, with .wal-tip. The state classes keep working as the fallback, so a browser without these features is no worse off than today.

    Colour

    Install

    There is no script on this stage. Every state you see is the browser's.

    • .is-scrolled becomes a scroll-driven animation on the masthead's rule.
    • .is-active becomes scroll-target-group, the browser's scroll-spy.
    • .is-dismissed becomes interestfor and popover="hint".
    • .is-open becomes a <dialog> opened with commandfor.

    New parts, 4 of 7

    Native parts

    A carousel, a select that shows real swatches, and a menu that knows where it fits. All three are the browser's own elements, styled by walnut, with no script and no library.

    Why you would use it
    These are the three parts a site most often installs a library for. The browser's versions work with a keyboard and a screen reader from the start.
    On your site
    <link rel="stylesheet" href="walnut-scroll.css">
<ul class="wal-carousel"><li>…</li></ul>

<select class="wal-select">
  <button><selectedcontent></selectedcontent></button>
  <option><i class="swatch"></i> Forest</option>
</select>

<button popovertarget="more">More</button>
<div id="more" popover class="wal-menu">…</div>
    Status
    Ships. Chrome and Edge draw all three today. Elsewhere the carousel is a plain swipeable row and the select is the system's own. The carousel's dots and arrows are in their own sheet, since Next.js and Vite cannot read them yet. Under Materials the menu and the picker are brass.

      The select recolours the stage from where it sits. Each dot under the carousel is its slide's lead colour.

      New parts, 5 of 7

      Shuffle

      Filter or sort a grid and every card glides to its new place. Cards that leave fold away and cards that arrive rise into the gaps.

      Why you would use it
      Catalogues, portfolios, shops and dashboards all filter. Without this the grid jumps and the reader has to find their place again.
      On your site
      <ul class="wal-shuffle" id="materials">…</ul>

walnut.shuffle(materials, () => showOnly("wood"));
<script src="walnut-motion.js" defer></script>
      Status
      Ships, with walnut.shuffle in walnut-motion.js. It is a view transition scoped to the grid, so the rest of the page stays live while it moves. Without view transitions the grid changes at once.

        New parts, 6 of 7

        Charts from the script

        Column and ring charts in plain HTML, drawn in the palette's five cues and made like furniture. The columns stand on a brass rail, each on a brass shoe like a sideboard's legs; the gridlines are inlaid stringing; the ring sits in a brass bezel with a tick at each quarter.

        Why you would use it
        A small chart on a landing page or a case study rarely justifies a charting library, and a library's chart never looks like the site. These are the site's colours and its materials, and a new palette regrades every chart.
        On your site
        <ol class="wal-chart" style="--max: 80">
  <li><b>May</b><span style="--v: 42">42</span><span style="--v: 18">18</span></li>
  <li><b>Jun</b><span style="--v: 48">48</span><span style="--v: 22">22</span></li>
</ol>
<ul class="wal-key"><li>Search</li><li>Links</li></ul>

<div class="wal-ring" style="--a: 58; --b: 34">58%<small>on a phone</small></div>
        Status
        Ships, pure CSS. The brass and the stringing are the same in every palette and both modes, so a chart reads as part of the furniture. A new --v moves its column; under Materials it strikes and rings.

        New parts, 7 of 7

        Prints like a catalogue

        Print a walnut page and it comes out as a catalogue sheet: sheet numbers, the address after every link, colour kept to the parts that carry meaning, and nothing that only makes sense on a screen.

        Why you would use it
        Résumés, case studies, recipes, invoices and documentation all get printed or saved as PDF, and most sites print as a broken screenshot. A walnut page would print well with nothing added.
        On your site
        /* nothing: it is in walnut's stylesheet.
   A running head of your own is one line: */
@page { @top-left { content: "Alex Kim, case studies"; } }
        Status
        Ships, in the stylesheet. Sheet numbers and running heads print from the page margins in Chrome and Edge; elsewhere the sheet prints without them.

        Where it lives

        In walnut.css now

        Every stage in this deck runs on the built framework: dist/walnut.css, and dist/walnut-motion.js for the gatefold and the shuffle. The deck styles only itself.

        Openings and materials

        1. Plan chest. .wal-chest on <details>. Pure CSS.
        2. Sliding door. .wal-door on <dialog>. Pure CSS.
        3. Gatefold. .wal-window with data-wal-open="gatefold", played by walnut-motion.js.
        4. Step inside. walnut.recolour with a change, from walnut-recolour.js.
        5. Materials. data-motion="materials" on the page, or data-wal-material on any part.

        Parts

        1. Corners. --wal-corner-shape; Catalogue bevels.
        2. Any colour. .wal-tint, .wal-fill-tint, --wal-ink() and --wal-soft().
        3. No script left. The nav's own state, and .wal-tip.
        4. Native parts. .wal-carousel, .wal-select and .wal-menu.
        5. Shuffle. .wal-shuffle and walnut.shuffle().
        6. Charts. .wal-chart, .wal-ring and .wal-key.
        7. Print. Nothing to add; it is in the stylesheet.

        Every class and token is in the reference, and the catalogue opens each colourway in a gatefold.
